Key to AP Gov success: Know your required SCOTUS cases AND foundational documents cold — the exam quotes primary sources and asks you to apply them. Understand the tensions built into the Constitution: federal vs. state power, majority rule vs. minority rights, executive power vs. Congress. Apply these tensions to current and historical events in your FRQs.
UNIT 01

Foundations of American Democracy

UNIT 1
Foundations of American Democracy
15–22% of Exam
Core Concepts
  • Natural rights philosophy (Locke: life, liberty, property → Jefferson: pursuit of happiness)
  • Social contract theory: people give up some freedoms for security/order
  • Popular sovereignty, limited government, separation of powers
  • Articles of Confederation: weaknesses (no tax, no army, unanimous amendment)
  • Constitutional Convention 1787: Great Compromise (bicameral Congress)
  • 3/5 Compromise (slavery), Electoral College design
  • Federalism: concurrent, enumerated, reserved, implied powers
  • Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists debate (see foundational docs)
Required Documents
  • Declaration of Independence — natural rights, grievances, popular sovereignty
  • Articles of Confederation — weak central gov, states retain power
  • Federalist No. 10 — Madison: factions, large republic as solution
  • Federalist No. 51 — Madison: separation of powers, checks & balances, "ambition must counteract ambition"
  • Brutus No. 1 — Anti-Federalist: federal power threatens liberty, standing army danger
  • Mayflower Compact, Virginia Plan, New Jersey Plan
FederalismConcurrent powersSupremacy ClauseFull Faith & CreditNecessary & ProperCommerce ClauseElastic Clause10th Amendment
Exam Focus

The FRQ almost always asks you to apply Federalist 10 or 51. Key argument: in a large republic, so many factions exist that no single one can dominate — this protects liberty better than small direct democracies. Brutus counters: large republic means citizens lose connection to government. Know both sides.

UNIT 2
Interactions Among the Branches
25–36% of Exam
Congress
  • Bicameral: House (435, proportional, 2yr) + Senate (100, 2/state, 6yr)
  • Enumerated powers: tax, declare war, regulate commerce, coin money
  • House: originates revenue bills, impeachment, Ways & Means Committee
  • Senate: confirms appointments, ratifies treaties, tries impeachment
  • Filibuster (Senate only) — requires 60 votes to invoke cloture
  • Pork barrel spending, logrolling, earmarks
  • Mandatory vs. discretionary spending; debt ceiling
President + Courts
  • Formal powers: veto, commander-in-chief, treaties, appointments, pardons
  • Informal powers: executive orders, signing statements, bully pulpit
  • War Powers Resolution (1973): 60-day limit without Congressional approval
  • Federal bureaucracy: iron triangles, regulatory agencies, spoils vs. merit
  • Federal courts: constitutional vs. statutory, district → appellate → SCOTUS
  • Judicial review (Marbury v. Madison) — not in Constitution!
  • Stare decisis vs. judicial activism vs. restraint
  • Required SCOTUS: Marbury, Baker v. Carr, Shaw v. Reno, US v. Lopez
Checks & balancesVetoOverride (2/3 vote)Pocket vetoExecutive orderJudicial reviewIron triangleConference committeeGerrymandering
Exam Focus

Largest unit on the exam. Key comparison: formal vs. informal presidential power. Baker v. Carr established "one person, one vote" (political question can be ruled on); Shaw v. Reno limits racial gerrymandering (racial classification must meet strict scrutiny). US v. Lopez = Congress overreached Commerce Clause (landmark federalism limit).

UNIT 3
Civil Liberties & Civil Rights
13–18% of Exam
Civil Liberties (Bill of Rights)
  • 1st Amend: speech (Tinker, Schenck), religion (Engel, Wisconsin v. Yoder), press (NYT v. US)
  • 2nd Amend: McDonald v. Chicago — 2nd incorporated to states
  • 4th Amend: unreasonable search; exclusionary rule
  • 5th Amend: self-incrimination, double jeopardy, due process
  • 6th Amend: Gideon v. Wainwright — right to counsel incorporated
  • Selective incorporation via 14th Amendment due process clause
  • Prior restraint: NYT v. US — government cannot stop press pre-publication
Civil Rights
  • 14th Amendment: equal protection, due process against states
  • Brown v. Board (1954): overturns Plessy "separate but equal"
  • Civil Rights Act 1964: bans discrimination by race, color, religion, sex, national origin
  • Voting Rights Act 1965: ends literacy tests, poll taxes
  • Affirmative action debates — strict scrutiny for racial classifications
  • Required doc: MLK Letter from Birmingham Jail — civil disobedience, moral law
  • Women's rights: 19th Amendment, Title IX, Reed v. Reed
Selective incorporationDue processEqual protectionPrior restraintClear & present dangerStrict scrutinyRational basisExclusionary rule
Exam Focus

Civil liberties (government vs. individual) vs. civil rights (equal treatment of groups) — know the difference. Tinker v. Des Moines = students have 1st Amendment rights in school (armbands = symbolic speech). Schenck = wartime limits speech. Wisconsin v. Yoder = religious freedom can override compulsory education law (Free Exercise). Engel v. Vitale = no school prayer (Establishment Clause).

UNIT 4
Political Ideologies & Beliefs
10–15% of Exam
Political Socialization
  • Family is #1 agent of political socialization
  • Other agents: peers, media, school, religion, events
  • Public opinion polling: sampling bias, wording effects, margin of error
  • Generational effects vs. lifecycle effects on ideology
  • Ideological spectrum: liberal → moderate → conservative
Ideology & Policy
  • Liberals: larger government, social safety net, regulated economy, expanded rights
  • Conservatives: smaller government, free market, traditional values, strong defense
  • Gender gap in voting: women lean more Democratic
  • Racial/ethnic group voting patterns and realignment
  • Linkage institutions: parties, interest groups, media, elections

UNIT 5
Political Participation
20–27% of Exam
Elections & Parties
  • Two-party system: plurality voting + winner-take-all creates Duverger's law
  • Primary elections: select party candidates; open vs. closed primaries
  • Electoral College: 538 electors, 270 to win; winner-take-all (most states)
  • Campaign finance: Citizens United v. FEC — unlimited independent expenditures
  • PACs, Super PACs, dark money 501(c)(4)s
  • Third parties: wasted vote problem, spoiler effect
Interest Groups & Media
  • Interest groups: lobbying, campaign donations, grassroots mobilization
  • Iron triangle: agency + committee + interest group = stable policy network
  • Collective action problem: free rider issue limits civic participation
  • Media: agenda setting, framing, priming — shapes what public thinks is important
  • Social media and political polarization
  • Voter turnout: highest education/income; presidential years higher than midterms
  • Voting rights history: 15th, 19th, 24th, 26th Amendments
Citizens UnitedSuper PACElectoral CollegeAgenda settingIron triangleFree riderVoter turnoutCollective action
Exam Focus

Citizens United v. FEC (2010) = corporations/unions have 1st Amendment rights → unlimited independent spending on political campaigns. Controversial because it equates money with speech. Know: how the Electoral College advantages swing states; how media framing differs from agenda setting (what to think ABOUT vs. how to think about it).

Required Foundational Documents — Quick Reference

Federalist No. 10
Author
James Madison, 1787
Argument
Factions are dangerous but inevitable. A large republic with representative government and separation of powers neutralizes faction better than small direct democracies.
Key Quote
"Liberty is to faction what air is to fire."
Federalist No. 51
Author
Madison, 1788
Argument
Separation of powers + checks and balances ensure no branch dominates. Federalism gives citizens double security. "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition."
Key Quote
"If men were angels, no government would be necessary."
Federalist No. 70
Author
Alexander Hamilton, 1788
Argument
Energy in the executive is essential. A single president is better than a council — unity, secrecy, decision, dispatch. Single executive is more accountable.
Key Quote
"Energy in the executive is a leading character in the definition of good government."
Federalist No. 78
Author
Hamilton, 1788
Argument
Judiciary is "least dangerous branch" — no sword (enforcement) or purse (spending). Life tenure protects judges from political pressure. Justifies judicial review.
Key Quote
"The courts were designed to be an intermediate body between the people and the legislature."
Brutus No. 1
Author
Robert Yates (?), 1787
Argument
Anti-Federalist warning: federal power under the new Constitution is too vast, will absorb state power, standing army threatens liberty, large republic means citizens lose government.
Key Quote
"It appears from these articles that there is no need of any intervention of the state governments."
Letter from Birmingham Jail
Author
Martin Luther King Jr., 1963
Argument
Unjust laws need not be obeyed — there is a moral law above civil law. Civil disobedience is justified when channels of change are closed. Urgency of "now."
Key Quote
"One has not only a legal but a moral responsibility to obey just laws. Conversely, one has a moral responsibility to disobey unjust laws."
Declaration of Independence
Author
Jefferson, 1776
Argument
Locke's natural rights. Government derives power from consent of governed. When government violates rights, people may abolish it.
Key Quote
"We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal…"
Articles of Confederation
Adopted
1777 (ratified 1781)
Weaknesses
No power to tax directly, no national currency, no executive branch, no ability to regulate commerce, unanimous vote required for amendments.
Why replaced
Shays' Rebellion showed weakness of central government.
